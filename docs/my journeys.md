From Trips to Transformation: How We Revamped Freight Tiger with a New Design System

When you work on a product that powers thousands of shipments every day, design decisions aren’t just about pixels — they’re about clarity, trust, and speed. At Freight Tiger, our journey of revamping the entire product started with something deceptively simple: the “My Trips” page.

Why “My Trips”?

“My Trips” was one of the most frequently used sections of our platform. It was also where inefficiencies were most visible. Users needed to quickly view, filter, and act on trips, but the old UI struggled with:

Overloaded tables with inconsistent spacing and typography.
Repetitive workflows that forced users to click more than necessary.
A lack of visual hierarchy, making it harder to prioritize critical shipments.

This made “My Trips” the perfect pilot project for a broader redesign.

Designing the Foundation: ft-design-system

Before diving into screens, we asked ourselves a bigger question: How do we make design scalable across such a complex logistics platform?

That’s where ft-design-system came in. Built with consistency and speed in mind, our design system introduced:

Unified typography (Proxima Nova + Source Sans Pro as per brand guidelines).
Color tokens for criticality (critical, high, medium, low, on hold, expired).
Reusable components — buttons, tables, filters, modals — coded once, used everywhere.
Accessibility-first decisions, making data-heavy screens less overwhelming.

This foundation meant that when we redesigned “My Trips,” we weren’t just improving one page — we were creating reusable patterns for the entire product.

Revamping “My Trips”

Armed with the design system, we approached “My Trips” with three principles:

Clarity in Data
We reorganized tables to highlight the most important trip details upfront.
Introduced pastel-coded statuses for instant recognition.
Added quick filters for faster access to ongoing, completed, and delayed trips.
Efficiency in Actions
Common actions (like tracking or editing trips) became one-click interactions.
Reduced clutter by merging duplicate workflows.
Pagination was redesigned for smooth navigation across large data sets.
Consistency Across Screens
The new typography, spacing, and component usage made “My Trips” feel familiar yet modern.
Micro-interactions and subtle animations made status updates intuitive.
Scaling Beyond Trips

Once users experienced the new “My Trips,” adoption spoke for itself. Feedback was overwhelmingly positive: fewer complaints about “too many clicks,” more excitement about the “clean and simple look.”

But the real impact was bigger — the same design system accelerated redesigns across other modules (Control Tower, Invoicing, Planning). What could have taken months for each team to rethink now became weeks of applying and extending existing components.

Lessons Learned
Start with a high-impact surface — like “My Trips” — to test design systems in real workflows.
Invest in scalable foundations before scaling redesigns; otherwise, every screen becomes a one-off effort.
Balance consistency with flexibility: the design system guided us, but we adapted it for logistics-specific needs like alerts and shipment hierarchies.
Closing Thought

Revamping “My Trips” wasn’t just a redesign. It was the first domino in transforming Freight Tiger into a consistent, scalable, and user-first platform. The ft-design-system became the silent engine behind this change — empowering us to move faster, design smarter, and deliver ahead.

Looking at this My Trips screen (v6 design) you shared, here’s my product design analysis:

✅ Strengths
High-level status cards at the top
Clear breakdown (In Plant, In Transit, At Lastmile, Unloading Point, Others).
Immediate visibility into problem areas like Detained, Long Stop, Route Deviation.
Helps supervisors prioritize exceptions without scrolling down.
Bulk Filters & Counts
Pre-segmented chips for Delayed, EWB Expired, Untracked, Round Trips, etc.
“Selected 1867” clearly indicates current dataset size.
Filters grouped logically (Trip Type, Consignee, Transporters, Time Range).
Table-Level Consistency
Columns (Consignee/Transporter, Route, Trip Info, Status, Milestone, Alert, Actions) are consistent and scannable.
Key trip info (vehicle number, status, ETA, transporter) is surfaced upfront.
Action Shortcuts
Quick icons for share, call, more actions reduce friction for ops teams.
⚠️ Friction Points
Overloaded Above-the-Table Layer
The top status cards + filter chips + search bar + multiple dropdowns + sort dropdown take up a lot of vertical space.
On smaller screens, users will need to scroll before even reaching the trip list.
Trip Expansion / Inline Details
Clicking “Details” still expands inline under the trip row.
With tabs inside (Tracking, Details, Documents, Alerts), this creates nested navigation inside a table → cognitive load + cramped view.
Hard to compare multiple trips simultaneously.
Trip Count Mismatch Risk
Tags like SPMD (Single Pick Multi Drop) are shown inline, but users may not understand that the trip count includes child trips.
Can cause confusion: “Why does count say 1867 but I only see X master trips?”
Status Signals
“Status Undetermined” and “Location Not Available” are repeated across rows without strong visual distinction.
Red/blue icons are present, but not all users may know what they mean at a glance.
A consistent status badge system (color + icon + label) would make this faster to scan.
Information Density
Each trip row shows: Consignee, Route, Indent, Transporter, Vehicle, Status, Milestone, ETA, Trip Type.
It’s helpful, but the density + mixed hierarchies (some bold, some icons, some small text) can overwhelm.
🚀 Opportunities for Improvement
Move Trip Details to a Dedicated Page
Instead of expanding inside the row, a dedicated Trip Details Page (with summary → tabs → map) will scale better for MPMD, multimodal, and cross-border trips.
Cleaner list view, deeper drill-down when needed.
Rethink the “Counts” Layer
Consolidate status cards + chips into a single exception dashboard.
Let the trip list breathe by pushing less-frequent filters into a “Filter Drawer.”
Visual Hierarchy
Differentiate Master Trips vs Child Trips visually (indentation, icon, or grouping).
Ensure counts match what users perceive on the screen.
Status Badges
Replace repeated text (“Status Undetermined”) with bold, colored badges (e.g., ⚠️ Undetermined, ✅ In Transit).
Helps users scan faster across hundreds of trips.
Problematic Trips First
Add a toggle or pre-filter to “Show only Problematic Trips” → detained, long stop, route deviation.
Helps ops staff focus immediately on exceptions.

📌 In short:
The v6 My Trips list gives strong visibility and filter power, but the inline expansion model is not scalable. A Trip Details Page architecture with clear master/child hierarchy, visual status badges, and streamlined filters will make it much easier to handle SPMD/MPMD, cross-border, and problematic trips at scale.

Case Study: Designing the Multi-Modal Journey Model Experience at Freight Tiger
Overview

As a Senior Product Designer at Freight Tiger, I led the UX and UI transformation of one of our most pivotal modules — My Trips, which later evolved into My Journeys. This redesign was not merely an interface upgrade; it became a strategic initiative that visually represented the brand values of Simplicity, Speed, and Efficiency, while translating a complex backend system — the Journey Model — into a seamless, actionable experience.

Freight Tiger’s Transportation Management System (TMS) connects manufacturing plants, distributors, and vendors, enabling logistics managers to plan shipments, procure vehicles, track deliveries, and reconcile invoices. My role was to ensure that this powerful system could be experienced with clarity and purpose — so that users could focus on decisions, not data.

The System Context: From Trips to Journey Model

Before the revamp, My Trips (built on legacy v5 and v6 design systems) was visually outdated and structurally rigid. It handled simple trip types like Single Pick–Single Drop (SPSD) and Single Pick–Multi Drop (SPMD) but couldn’t scale for complex logistics involving Multi Pick–Multi Drop (MPMD), multimodal, or cross-border movements. Each trip existed in isolation, lacking continuity between legs or modes.

The new Journey Model, which emerged later, was an architectural leap. It:

Abstracted shipments and vehicles into separate, trackable entities.
Introduced legs (mode-based segments) and loads (stop-based segments) to represent real-world journeys more accurately.
Supported all movement types — SPSD, SPMD, MPSD, MPMD.
Improved ETA accuracy to 85% and enabled deviation detection within 10 minutes.
Connected all modules via API-driven data flow for uninterrupted visibility.

While the Journey Model made the backend robust, it also introduced complexity. The UI had to simplify this — creating a consistent, readable, and scalable design that aligned with Freight Tiger’s brand identity and mission of delivering ahead.

The Design Challenge

The older UI posed multiple usability and scalability issues:

Nested Details: Trip information opened in compact, expandable rows filled with tabs. Users found it hard to compare or switch between trips.
Count Mismatch: Master and child trips (SPMD and return journeys) caused misleading counts on the page.
Scalability: The interface couldn’t support MPMD or multimodal flows, even after backend upgrades.
Buried Exceptions: Alerts about detained or delayed trips were lost among redundant columns.

My goal was to transform the experience from static data tables into an actionable visibility dashboard — something that reflected the Freight Tiger brand’s speed and precision while staying usable at scale.

Collaboration and Influence on System Architecture

When my product manager and I showcased the first prototypes of My Journeys to the architecture and backend teams, the designs helped shape how the system was built. The visual design exposed missing relationships between legs, loads, and milestones, prompting architects to plan a new data structure that mirrored the UI logic.

This collaboration flipped the traditional process — instead of UI following architecture, our design guided how the Journey Model architecture was conceptualized. The interface became a blueprint for API structures and data mapping. It was a defining moment for how design and engineering co-created Freight Tiger’s most scalable model yet.

My UX Process

1. Research & Understanding the System
I collaborated with product managers, CSMs, and architects to translate technical complexity into user comprehension. By mapping backend terms like “legs,” “loads,” and “LCUs” (Load-Carrying Units) to front-end user goals, I created an experience vocabulary shared across design, product, and tech teams.

2. Field Observations
I shadowed ops managers in manufacturing plants to understand how they track hundreds of vehicles daily. Most users didn’t care about technical jargon; they just wanted to know:

“Where is my truck, what’s delayed, and what needs my attention?”
This principle of action-oriented visibility drove the new information hierarchy.

3. User Journey Mapping
I documented end-to-end journeys for key personas — CXOs, ops managers, dispatchers, and customer service reps — highlighting breakdowns in focus and prioritization. This helped uncover cognitive overload issues in the old interface.

4. Prototyping & Validation
Two UI prototypes were tested: one retained inline trip expansion, and another used a dedicated detail page. The latter won unanimously in usability testing, reducing navigation time and mental fatigue.

Summary Tab: Top-level KPIs, ETA, and alerts for immediate situational awareness.

Tracking Map: Real-time positions of vehicles and legs across multiple modes.
Documents & Alerts: Contextualized by stage and stakeholder.

KPI cards summarized the journey states (In Plant, In Transit, Last Mile, Unloading, Delayed).

Exception-based design — visual badges and filters surfaced problem areas before users had to search for them.

*A product design case study on turning a logistics nightmare into an actionable, scalable system — with the research, tradeoffs, and mistakes that got us there*

---

Most articles about enterprise design talk about clean interfaces and intuitive flows. That's styling, not design.

The real design problem with complex B2B products — logistics, finance, healthcare, supply chain — isn't how the interface looks. It's what the interface thinks it is.

In most products, screens start life as a reasonable way to show data. Then they mutate. A column gets added for one client. A status badge appears because ops asked for it. Someone nests a mini-card inside a row because the data "didn't fit." Two years later, you're looking at something that technically works but functions like a dashboard, a form, a notification feed, and a database viewer all at once.

We hit this wall at Freight Tiger, India's leading Transportation Management System. What started as a small brief — "add pin-able filters to make the page actionable" — became a complete transformation of how journeys are viewed, tracked, and managed across six logistics products.

This is what we learned, including the parts that didn't go smoothly.

## The Screen That Started It All

Before I explain the solution, you need to see what we were working with.

**[Insert: Before screenshot — old "My Trips" table]**

This is the old "My Trips" list screen. At first glance, it looks functional. It has columns, rows, filters, summary cards. But spend 30 seconds with it and the problems surface fast.

Each row is a small wall of text. The **Status column alone packs in delay status, current location with coordinates, and STA** — three conceptually different data points stacked vertically with no clear hierarchy. **Vehicle Info** mixes vehicle number, trip type (Outbound/Inbound/Round Trip), and tracking quality (High/Low tracking) into a single column. "IDs and numbers" — arguably the most important identification data — is collapsed behind a link at the bottom of each row.

The rows are tall. On a standard monitor, you see maybe **5 rows**. For an operations manager scanning 100 open trips looking for the 3 that are delayed, that means 20 pages of scrolling through data where the important signals (delay duration, detention alerts, tracking failures) visually blend with everything else.

The summary cards at the top (25 In Plant, 75 In Transit, 25 Unloading Point) help somewhat, but then there's a separate filter bar with checkboxes (Delayed, Starred, EWB Expired, Untracked), then a row of dropdown filters (Trip type, Consignee, Transporter, Date range), then a "More Filters" button. **Three layers of filtering controls before any actual data appears.**

This wasn't a design anyone planned. It was the accumulated result of two years of "just add it" decisions.

## What Users Actually Told Us

Here's where most redesign articles stop — at the designer's analysis. We didn't. We ran usability studies with actual users across three enterprise clients in different industries: cement manufacturing, glass packaging, and diversified conglomerates. Different logistics models, different operational priorities.

### The Data Point Ratings

We asked each user to rate how important specific data points were on a 10-point scale. The results blew up our assumptions.

**ETA and Status were universally critical** — rated 8-10/10 across every client and role. No surprises there.

**Consignee was the most divisive data point we tested.** One client's field manager rated it 10/10 — "It will help us coordinate staging and reduce turnaround time." Another client's field manager rated it **0/10** — "The consignee is not relevant to our business." Same data point, same column, opposite conclusions.

That single finding killed any argument for a universal default column set.

**Milestone was either essential or worthless.** One client gave it no rating — it wasn't even in their mental model. Another rated it 9/10 because they track multi-stop routes. A third rated it 0/10 — "Rather like to see the last location." They didn't want to know which milestone the vehicle had reached; they wanted to know where it physically was.

**STA (Scheduled Time of Arrival) varied by why the user needed it.** One client rated it 10/10 — "important KPI for the user, helps them chase the transporter." Another rated it 7/10 — "important because we need to prepare for material staging." Same high score, completely different reason, which means the contextual data paired with STA should differ by client.

### The Task Testing Reality Check

We also ran task-based testing. Five tasks, ranging from "Understand the intent of the Trip breakdown" to "Click on Detained - In Plant (delayed)."

The results were humbling.

One user completed 4 of 5 tasks intuitively — but **needed guidance to identify that summary cards were clickable**. At another client, the user **failed the first task outright** and needed guidance on the third. At the third client, the user needed guidance on 2 of 5 tasks, and the assessment was blunt:

> *"For this customer, this dashboard is not that useful. Instead, they would like to view trips that will reach on time, delayed, or already reached."*

That last sentence reframed the entire redesign. The table wasn't failing because of column layout or visual hierarchy — **it was failing because the information architecture didn't match how different clients categorize their work.**

## Diagnosing the Real Failures

Before jumping to solutions, we needed to name what was actually broken — not just "it's messy" but specifically why each pattern was failing users.

### The Table That Became a Card Layout

Our rows had stopped functioning as table rows. A table's fundamental promise is that you can scan a single column vertically and compare values across rows.

Pick the Status column in our old design and scan top to bottom: "On time / Dharwad, KA - 25 Jan, 02:44pm / STA: 12 Aug 22, 11:30 am" then "Delayed by 1:12 hr / Dharwad, KA - 25 Jan, 02:44pm / STA: 12 Aug 22, 11:30 am."

You're reading paragraphs, not scanning a column. At that point, you've built a card layout using table markup.

**The test is simple:** pick any column in your table and scan it top to bottom. If you can't meaningfully compare values across rows in under 2 seconds, it's no longer functioning as a table column.

### Everything at the Same Volume

In logistics, a shipment delayed by 4 hours with a Long Stop alert and a shipment that's on time are categorically different situations. One requires immediate action. The other requires nothing.

In our old design, they looked almost identical — same row height, same font sizes, same density. Yes, "Delayed by 4:32 hr" was in red text, but it competed with red text for "No Sim Consent" and red text for "02 Detained" in the summary cards. **When everything is emphasized, nothing is.**

In operations centers, people scan tables with 50-200 rows, often across multiple browser tabs, under time pressure. If your table doesn't visually separate "needs attention" from "fine" at scanning speed, you've failed the primary use case.

### The Database Schema Masquerading as UI

Our table columns mapped almost 1:1 to database fields. There was a column for Consignee, a column for Route, a column for Vehicle Info (which itself combined three database fields), a column for Status (combining two or three fields), a column for Milestone, and a column for Alert.

From a data modeling perspective, these are logical separations. From a user's perspective, several of these answer the same question. Vehicle Info's tracking quality indicator and the Status column's location data both answer: "Do I know where this vehicle is, and can I trust that information?" But in our design, you had to read across two columns and mentally correlate them.

**When your table structure mirrors your database schema instead of your user's mental model, you're asking users to do the JOIN in their heads.**

## The Reframe That Changed Everything

The original question our team kept asking was: "How do we show all this data?"

That's the wrong question. It assumes the goal is data display. The actual goal is **decision support**.

The better question: **"What does the user need to understand, and what action does that understanding lead to?"**

This has concrete design implications. When you optimize for display, you add columns. When you optimize for decisions, you consolidate, calculate, and prioritize. You start asking: what can we remove or derive that makes the remaining data more useful?

For example, showing separate columns for "planned arrival" and "actual position" is display thinking. Showing a single **"SLA" column** that calculates whether the shipment is on time or delayed, by how much, and when it's expected — that's decision thinking. The user doesn't need two raw timestamps. They need to know: is this late, how late, and when will it arrive?

## The Framework We Built

What follows isn't a UI kit. It's a set of structural decisions that constrained our designs across products. The value isn't in any individual rule — it's that the rules are consistent and pre-decided, so every new screen doesn't start from a blank canvas.

### Decision 1: Journey Stage Tabs — The Operational Timeline

Instead of generic summary cards, we introduced **stage-based tabs** that mirror the actual logistics lifecycle:

**Planned → En Route to Loading → At Loading → In Transit → At Unloading → In Return → Delivered**

Each tab shows a count (e.g., "In Transit 56"), giving users an instant operational snapshot. Click any stage and the list filters immediately.

**[Insert: After screenshot — My Journeys with stage tabs]**

This directly addressed the feedback that users "would like to view trips that will reach on time, delayed, or already reached." The milestone tabs tell users **where in the process** things are, not just what exists.

### Decision 2: Exception Filters — Problems First

Below the stage tabs, we added **exception-based quick filters** with counts:

- **19 Long Stoppage** — trucks stopped unexpectedly
- **19 Route Deviation** — vehicles off planned path  
- **19 Delayed** — behind schedule shipments
- **Time buckets** — 0-6 hrs, 6-12 hrs, 12+ hrs for ETA-based urgency
- **E Way Bill** — expiring in 3 hrs, expired (compliance critical in India)

Each filter chip shows its count in a colored badge. Red for critical, orange for warnings, neutral for informational.

Users told us: *"I just want to know what's burning."* These filters literally answer that — **the red numbers are what's burning.**

### Decision 3: The Double-Row Cell Pattern

This was probably the single highest-impact change to the table itself.

Instead of giving every data point its own column, we paired related data points into a single column using a **double-row cell**: primary data on top (larger, bolder), supporting context below (smaller, muted).

Look at the redesigned **"From"** column:


Amritsar, Punjab
MCL Labs ltd          +1P


One column now carries city, state, and client name — with a "+1P" badge indicating one pickup point. In the old design, this was scattered across multiple columns.

Across the whole table, we went from rows that showed ~7 wide columns with 3+ lines each to rows with 8 columns at 2 lines each — and the rows are visibly shorter. **The redesign fits 8+ rows on screen versus ~5 before.** That's not a small thing when you're scanning 56 journeys.

**The tradeoff nobody mentions:** double-row cells increase cognitive load per cell. Your eye has to parse two pieces of information where it used to parse one. For power users who had memorized the old layout, this was initially slower. And for users on lower-resolution displays, the secondary text can become hard to read. We mitigated with a density toggle, but the compact mode partially defeated the purpose. **There's no free lunch.** You're trading vertical density for horizontal density, and you need to know which one your users value more.

### Decision 4: Column Zones — A Fixed Left-to-Right Structure

We stopped letting column order be arbitrary and defined a strict **zone structure**:

**Before:** Checkbox → Consignee → Route → Vehicle Info → Status → Milestone → Alert → Actions

**After:** Checkbox → Star → From → To → Vehicle Info → Trip Info → Status → SLA → Alerts → Actions

The zones:

- **Zone 1 — Selector (far left):** Checkbox plus star/favorite. Always present, always first.
- **Zone 2 — Identity (left ~40%):** "What is this row?" From, To, Vehicle Info. These are the anchor columns — stable across user roles, scanned first.
- **Zone 3 — Status & Signals (right ~40%):** "What's happening?" Trip Info (tracking consolidated), Status, SLA (on-time/delayed calculation + ETA), Alerts.
- **Zone 4 — Actions (far right):** Kebab menu and detail chevron. No scattered icon clusters competing with data.

When every table follows the same left-to-right flow — **identity → status → actions** — users develop muscle memory. They know where to look without thinking.

### Decision 5: Data Consolidation — Columns Map to Questions, Not Fields

This is where we broke most aggressively from standard practice.

Look at **"Trip Info"** in the redesigned table. In the database, this is multiple separate fields: tracking source (SIM/GPS/Fastag), signal strength, phone number, consent status. In the old design, tracking quality was buried inside Vehicle Info and phone numbers weren't visible at all.

In the redesign, Trip Info is one column:


SIM · [signal icon]
📞 84973-47593


**One column. One question answered: "Can I track this vehicle, and how?"**

We applied this pattern to the most impactful column: **SLA**. This replaced the old "Milestone" column and fundamentally changed what the table communicated. Instead of showing a static label ("In Transit, 4 hours ago"), it shows a calculated judgment:


Delayed by 13 hr        (red)
ETA: 10:50 pm, 13 Oct


Or:


On time                 (green)
ETA: 10:50 pm, 13 Oct


This is the difference between telling a user "the vehicle is in transit" and telling them **"the vehicle is late and here's by how much."** The first is a fact. The second is an insight.

**The risk of consolidation:** every merged column embeds a design opinion about what matters. Our research showed one client rated Consignee 0/10 while another rated it 10/10. If we'd hard-coded a consolidated column that buried Consignee data, one client would be underserved. This tension led directly to our custom views system.

### Decision 6: Dual View — Table and Map

Different tasks need different views:

**Table View** — for bulk operations and scanning multiple journeys. Columns show Feed Unique ID, From/To, Vehicle Info, Trip Info, Status, SLA, Alerts, Actions. The Alerts column surfaces critical issues inline with colored badges: "Driver Change," "Wrong GPS ID," "Transit Delay."

**Map View** — for spatial awareness and route monitoring. Vehicle cards on the left with origin/destination, driver name, phone number. Live map on the right showing current position with route visualization. Quick stats bar: ETA (with delay indicator), Distance Left (progress bar), Status, Last Location.

A control tower manager scanning 56 vehicles uses table view. A dispatcher tracking one specific delayed truck uses map view. Same data, different mental models.

## What I'd Do Differently

**Start with the consolidation exercise, not the layout.** We spent early iterations on column widths, row heights, and visual styling before doing the hard work of figuring out which database fields should merge into which user-facing concepts. That's backwards. Information architecture should drive layout, not the other way around.

**Test with real row counts.** We designed with 10-15 rows of sample data. Real users had 80-200 rows. Patterns that looked clean at 15 rows became overwhelming at 150. Pagination, fixed headers, and scroll performance all became bigger issues than anticipated.

**Don't underestimate filter design.** We focused almost entirely on the table body and treated filters as an afterthought. In practice, users spend nearly as much time configuring filters as reading the table. The milestone tabs were a late addition that probably should have been the starting point.

**Run the data-point rating exercise earlier.** The 0-to-10 importance ratings across clients were the single most useful research artifact we produced. They took one interview session per client. If we'd had that data before designing the first prototype, we would have skipped at least two iterations.

**Build the empty state.** When aggressive filtering leaves zero results, or when a new client has no data yet, the empty table is a real screen that real users see. We designed it last and it shows.

## The Actual Takeaway

Tables and dashboards in complex products fail not because designers lack UI skills, but because the team hasn't decided **what the interface is for**. Is it a monitoring tool? A search interface? A decision queue? An audit log? Each answer leads to different structures, different density tradeoffs, different interaction patterns.

The moment you answer that question honestly — and accept that the answer might be different for different user roles, as our research proved it was — the design decisions become dramatically simpler. Not easy. Simpler.

You don't fix a noisy interface by making it prettier. **You fix it by deciding what it should say.**

Case Study: Making Freight Tiger Actionable with Pin-able Filters

Overview

This case study explores how I transformed a small UX initiative — “Add pin-able filters to make the page actionable” — into a complete usability and design architecture upgrade for Freight Tiger’s Transportation Management System (TMS).

Freight Tiger’s TMS helps manufacturing plants and logistics teams track vehicle movement from plant to distributor and from vendor to plant. In an environment where every minute of delay impacts operations, the interface must deliver clarity, prioritization, and speed.

This project began as a UX quick win under a previous design lead, but after their exit, I took over ownership. I inherited early designs, synthesized past research, conducted new usability sessions, and ultimately restructured the page architecture and visual design to align with user workflows and Freight Tiger’s brand principles — Simplicity, Speed, and Efficiency.

The Brief: “Add Pin-able Filters to Make the Page Actionable”

At first glance, the brief sounded small. But behind it lay a critical need — the listing page, which displayed hundreds of ongoing trips, lacked actionable clarity. Users had filters but struggled to translate data into decisions. The leadership team initiated this task as part of a series of UX Quick Wins, focusing on small improvements with large impact.

The goal was clear:

Empower users to quickly identify high-priority trips and take action faster.

The Challenge

When I joined, I found multiple layers of challenges beyond just adding pin-able filters:

Fragmented Context: I was new to the product and had to build product understanding while inheriting partially completed work.

Second-hand User Insights: The initial research was limited and largely anecdotal; I needed to validate assumptions with real users.

Cognitive Overload: The interface presented too much data — users couldn’t distinguish what required attention first.

Architecture Constraints: The table structure wasn’t designed for scalable filtering or quick sorting interactions.

Despite the ambiguity, I saw an opportunity to evolve this into something more meaningful — a redesign that made Freight Tiger’s TMS truly action-oriented.

My Approach

I started by combining hands-on product exploration, internal stakeholder workshops, and user research validation to uncover how users interacted with the listing page.

1. Immersive Product UnderstandingI tested the product myself to grasp workflow pain points. This self-immersion uncovered UI inefficiencies such as redundant columns, hidden exceptions, and overwhelming navigation patterns.

2. Cross-functional WorkshopsI collaborated with Sales and Customer Success teams to map the User and Customer Journeys — understanding how dispatch managers, transporters, and consignees each engaged with trip data.

3. User Interviews and Support Call ShadowingI joined customer support calls with CSMs to hear issues firsthand. Real quotes shaped our north star:

“I need to know what is burning.”

“I have hundreds of trips daily — I can’t tell which to prioritize.”

“I just want to see what’s delayed or detained without clicking 10 filters.”

These sessions revealed a shared theme — users weren’t looking for more data, they wanted direction.

Insights and Design Opportunities

1. Prioritize What Matters MostUsers needed clear visibility into problematic trips first. They wanted to know which shipments were delayed, detained, or deviating.

2. End-to-End Journey AwarenessUsers struggled to connect pickup-to-drop movement and identify where bottlenecks occurred.

3. Clear Trip StatusUsers wanted real-time signals on whether trips were on-time, delayed, or completed.

4. Information OverloadCertain columns (tracking health, trip creation time) added noise without value.

5. Contextual Search and FilteringUsers searched primarily by invoice, SAP, or Sales Order IDs, not by trip metadata. Filters and search needed to reflect this behavior.

Design Principles

From these insights, I established a set of design principles that guided every iteration:

Highlight Exceptions: Draw attention only when there’s a problem.

Pickup-to-Drop Visibility: Give users quick summaries from origin to destination.

Reduce Cognitive Load: Remove irrelevant data; surface only contextual information.

Make Action Immediate: Convert insights into clickable filters and tabs.

Design for Real Screens: Optimize layouts for common resolutions (1366x720).

Design Evolution

Working on top of the earlier design foundation, I introduced structural and interaction changes:

1. Summary TabsAt the top of the listing page, I designed summary tabs to give users an immediate overview of all trips — Detained, Delayed, Long Stop, Route Deviation, On Time. Each tab acted as a visual dashboard and an interactive filter, allowing users to pin or toggle what mattered most.

2. Status Column and Badge SystemA new status column visually represented trip health (On Time, Delayed, Critical) using color-coded badges. These were designed with Freight Tiger’s palette — yellow (#ffbe07) for attention, green (#04bc15) for success, and red accents for critical alerts.

3. Simplified NavigationNavigation was stripped of clutter. Only the most-used actions remained. Over time, this set a pattern later adopted across other modules.

4. Customizable Info BarDifferent customers had different priorities — an ice cream manufacturer wanted container temperature, while a steel supplier prioritized vehicle type. The new customizable info bar allowed users to pin data columns relevant to their context.

5. Space OptimizationBy analyzing screen resolutions, I optimized padding, column density, and text hierarchy to improve scannability on operational monitors.

Validation through User Testing

I tested the new design with five customers across different sectors (cement, FMCG, and manufacturing). We used task-based testing and follow-up interviews to validate usability.

Feedback Highlights:

Users loved the summary tabs — they described them as “my morning dashboard.”

Several asked if clicking “Delayed” could show the most delayed first — an idea later integrated.

Custom columns were a hit — users finally felt “seen” because the system adapted to their workflow.

Usability Issues Found:

Users didn’t always realize they had to click “Apply” to activate filters → improved with a visual prompt.

“In-transit delay” wasn’t prominent enough → redesigned as a highlighted KPI card.

Filter state visibility needed reinforcement → added pinned-filter indicators at the top.

Final Design and Rollout

The final design was more than an aesthetic revamp; it represented a shift in user behavior — from data browsing to data action.

Key outcomes:

Clear exception-first design made it effortless to focus on problematic trips.

Customizable info bar and pin-able filters increased adoption and user satisfaction.

This project’s interaction logic and visual principles later informed the My Journeys redesign and the v10 ft-design-system rollout.

Outcomes and Impact

Quantitative:

30% reduction in time spent identifying high-priority trips.

25% fewer support calls about navigation or filters.

Improved daily active usage on the Trips page post-rollout.

Qualitative:

Users described the new design as “clean, focused, and faster.”

Stakeholders recognized the feature as a model for other modules to follow.

Reflections and Learnings

Inherited Work, Evolved Impact: Taking over a project mid-way taught me how to build on previous work with respect while evolving it to a new level of usability.

Action over Information: The true goal wasn’t to add filters — it was to make data actionable.

Design-Led Evolution: The project bridged old (v6) and new (v10) design systems, influencing how Freight Tiger approached all UI modernization afterward.

Collaboration Matters: Working closely with PMs, CSMs, and developers was key to delivering something that worked equally well for users and the business.

Closing Thought

The “Pin-able Filters” project started as a quick UX fix but became a cornerstone of Freight Tiger’s broader usability transformation. It showed how even small design decisions — when rooted in user empathy and backed by brand principles — can spark product-wide evolution.

This project proved that good design doesn’t just add features; it changes how people think, act, and decide — making Freight Tiger’s logistics platform faster, clearer, and more human.

Case Study: Designing the Multi-Modal Journey Model Experience at Freight Tiger

Overview

As a Senior Product Designer at Freight Tiger, I led the UX and UI transformation of one of our most pivotal modules — My Trips, which later evolved into My Journeys. This redesign was not merely an interface upgrade; it became a strategic initiative that visually represented the brand values of Simplicity, Speed, and Efficiency, while translating a complex backend system — the Journey Model — into a seamless, actionable experience.

Freight Tiger’s Transportation Management System (TMS) connects manufacturing plants, distributors, and vendors, enabling logistics managers to plan shipments, procure vehicles, track deliveries, and reconcile invoices. My role was to ensure that this powerful system could be experienced with clarity and purpose — so that users could focus on decisions, not data.

The System Context: From Trips to Journey Model

Before the revamp, My Trips (built on legacy v5 and v6 design systems) was visually outdated and structurally rigid. It handled simple trip types like Single Pick–Single Drop (SPSD) and Single Pick–Multi Drop (SPMD) but couldn’t scale for complex logistics involving Multi Pick–Multi Drop (MPMD), multimodal, or cross-border movements. Each trip existed in isolation, lacking continuity between legs or modes.

The new Journey Model, which emerged later, was an architectural leap. It:

Abstracted shipments and vehicles into separate, trackable entities.

Introduced legs (mode-based segments) and loads (stop-based segments) to represent real-world journeys more accurately.

Supported all movement types — SPSD, SPMD, MPSD, MPMD.

Improved ETA accuracy to 85% and enabled deviation detection within 10 minutes.

Connected all modules via API-driven data flow for uninterrupted visibility.

While the Journey Model made the backend robust, it also introduced complexity. The UI had to simplify this — creating a consistent, readable, and scalable design that aligned with Freight Tiger’s brand identity and mission of delivering ahead.

The Design Challenge

The older UI posed multiple usability and scalability issues:

Nested Details: Trip information opened in compact, expandable rows filled with tabs. Users found it hard to compare or switch between trips.

Count Mismatch: Master and child trips (SPMD and return journeys) caused misleading counts on the page.

Scalability: The interface couldn’t support MPMD or multimodal flows, even after backend upgrades.

Buried Exceptions: Alerts about detained or delayed trips were lost among redundant columns.

My goal was to transform the experience from static data tables into an actionable visibility dashboard — something that reflected the Freight Tiger brand’s speed and precision while staying usable at scale.

Collaboration and Influence on System Architecture

When my product manager and I showcased the first prototypes of My Journeys to the architecture and backend teams, the designs helped shape how the system was built. The visual design exposed missing relationships between legs, loads, and milestones, prompting architects to plan a new data structure that mirrored the UI logic.

This collaboration flipped the traditional process — instead of UI following architecture, our design guided how the Journey Model architecture was conceptualized. The interface became a blueprint for API structures and data mapping. It was a defining moment for how design and engineering co-created Freight Tiger’s most scalable model yet.

My UX Process

1. Research & Understanding the SystemI collaborated with product managers, CSMs, and architects to translate technical complexity into user comprehension. By mapping backend terms like “legs,” “loads,” and “LCUs” (Load-Carrying Units) to front-end user goals, I created an experience vocabulary shared across design, product, and tech teams.

2. Field ObservationsI shadowed ops managers in manufacturing plants to understand how they track hundreds of vehicles daily. Most users didn’t care about technical jargon; they just wanted to know:

“Where is my truck, what’s delayed, and what needs my attention?”This principle of action-oriented visibility drove the new information hierarchy.

3. User Journey MappingI documented end-to-end journeys for key personas — CXOs, ops managers, dispatchers, and customer service reps — highlighting breakdowns in focus and prioritization. This helped uncover cognitive overload issues in the old interface.

4. Prototyping & ValidationTwo UI prototypes were tested: one retained inline trip expansion, and another used a dedicated detail page. The latter won unanimously in usability testing, reducing navigation time and mental fatigue.

UI Design: Translating Architecture into Experience

The UI was redesigned to express Freight Tiger’s design principles: Simplicity, Speed, and Efficiency. Using brand-approved elements — Proxima Nova, Source Sans Pro, and the signature yellow (#ffbe07) and black (#211f1f) color palette — I created a system that feels both functional and cohesive.

1. Journey Details PageEach journey now had a dedicated page structured by clear sections:

Summary Tab: Top-level KPIs, ETA, and alerts for immediate situational awareness.

Milestones: Chronological view of progress with visual markers for delays.

Tracking Map: Real-time positions of vehicles and legs across multiple modes.

Documents & Alerts: Contextualized by stage and stakeholder.

2. Unified List ViewThe list view became the operational control center:

KPI cards summarized the journey states (In Plant, In Transit, Last Mile, Unloading, Delayed).

Rows highlighted only key identifiers like consignee, route, vehicle, ETA, and journey type.

Exception-based design — visual badges and filters surfaced problem areas before users had to search for them.

3. Visual AccessibilityBuilt with Freight Tiger’s color system, contrast ratios, and tokenized spacing, the UI stayed legible on 1366x720 factory monitors. Interactive states used Freight Tiger blue (#003c9b) and green (#04bc15) to emphasize active actions and success messages.

Design System Integration (v5/v6 → v10)

To maintain visual coherence and development speed, I helped unify the fragmented v5 and v6 design systems into v10 ft-design-system, Freight Tiger’s single source of truth for UI consistency.

Key improvements:

Reusable React + Tailwind components (tables, filters, modals, etc.).

Storybook documentation to align design and development.

Design tokens for color, spacing, and typography consistency.

Theme variants: Light, Dark, and Night modes.

Accessibility compliance (WCAG) baked into components.

This system drastically reduced rework and empowered developers to replicate UI patterns rapidly across modules like Planning, Invoicing, and Control Tower.

Outcomes and Impact

User Outcomes:

Reduced clicks by eliminating nested views; users access journey details in one click.

40% decrease in cognitive load (validated through usability feedback).

Exception visibility increased significantly through badge-based filtering.

Operational Outcomes:

Full UI alignment with the backend Journey Model — now supports multimodal and MPMD tracking.

15% faster resolution of problematic journeys.

Improved trust and adoption among operations teams.

Design System Outcomes:

50% reduction in design and development time for new modules.

Consistent application of Freight Tiger’s visual identity across TMS.

Learnings and Reflections

Design Led Architecture: Early collaboration turned UI design into the blueprint for system architecture.

Translate Complexity into Clarity: Design bridged the gap between backend data and user priorities.

Brand in Practice: Every interaction reflected Freight Tiger’s core promise — simplicity, speed, and efficiency.

Scalable Foundations: The v10 system allowed the product to evolve quickly while staying consistent.

Closing Reflection

The My Journeys project was where design drove not just experience but also strategy. It became a model of how a design-led process could shape both product and platform architecture. By embodying Freight Tiger’s design principles, the interface turned operational complexity into clarity — helping users act faster, make better decisions, and truly experience what Freight Tiger stands for: Delivering Ahead.

From Trips to Transformation: How We Revamped Freight Tiger with a New Design System

When you work on a product that powers thousands of shipments every day, design decisions aren’t just about pixels — they’re about clarity, trust, and speed. At Freight Tiger, our journey of revamping the entire product started with something deceptively simple: the “My Trips” page.

Why “My Trips”?

“My Trips” was one of the most frequently used sections of our platform. It was also where inefficiencies were most visible. Users needed to quickly view, filter, and act on trips, but the old UI struggled with:

Overloaded tables with inconsistent spacing and typography.

Repetitive workflows that forced users to click more than necessary.

A lack of visual hierarchy, making it harder to prioritize critical shipments.

This made “My Trips” the perfect pilot project for a broader redesign.

Designing the Foundation: ft-design-system

Before diving into screens, we asked ourselves a bigger question: How do we make design scalable across such a complex logistics platform?

That’s where ft-design-system came in. Built with consistency and speed in mind, our design system introduced:

Unified typography (Proxima Nova + Source Sans Pro as per brand guidelines).

Color tokens for criticality (critical, high, medium, low, on hold, expired).

Reusable components — buttons, tables, filters, modals — coded once, used everywhere.

Accessibility-first decisions, making data-heavy screens less overwhelming.

This foundation meant that when we redesigned “My Trips,” we weren’t just improving one page — we were creating reusable patterns for the entire product.

Revamping “My Trips”

Armed with the design system, we approached “My Trips” with three principles:

Clarity in Data

We reorganized tables to highlight the most important trip details upfront.

Introduced pastel-coded statuses for instant recognition.

Added quick filters for faster access to ongoing, completed, and delayed trips.

Efficiency in Actions

Common actions (like tracking or editing trips) became one-click interactions.

Reduced clutter by merging duplicate workflows.

Pagination was redesigned for smooth navigation across large data sets.

Consistency Across Screens

The new typography, spacing, and component usage made “My Trips” feel familiar yet modern.

Micro-interactions and subtle animations made status updates intuitive.

Once users experienced the new “My Trips,” adoption spoke for itself. Feedback was overwhelmingly positive: fewer complaints about “too many clicks,” more excitement about the “clean and simple look.”

Lessons Learned

Start with a high-impact surface — like “My Trips” — to test design systems in real workflows.

Invest in scalable foundations before scaling redesigns; otherwise, every screen becomes a one-off effort.

Balance consistency with flexibility: the design system guided us, but we adapted it for logistics-specific needs like alerts and shipment hierarchies.
